## Identity

DFB Solutions is the umbrella commercial/problem-solving business for Drew's creative, technical, operational, media, automation, software, transportation, and business-support capabilities.

Tagline: DFB Solutions — for every problem we have the solution.

DFB Solutions should not be boxed into one industry. Its advantage is combining capabilities to solve useful real-world problems. The customer-facing business should be simpler than the internal capability map.

The long-term shift is:
Drew doing jobs -> DFB selling solutions -> DFB operating systems -> recurring revenue -> scalable delivery.

Potential recurring services include site/system maintenance, updates, automation monitoring, hosting/infrastructure support, content updates, reporting, managed AI/business operations, and ongoing optimization.

### Content Systems / Social Automation — growth offer
DFB Social OS is internal infrastructure. DFB may sell services built with it, such as planning, repurposing, branded content systems, review workflows, and managed content operations. The internal platform is not automatically a SaaS product.

### Creative AI Production — growth offer
DFB AI Studio is internal infrastructure. Sellable outputs may include AI-assisted commercials, animation, character content, music visuals, rapid visual concepts, stylized promotional work, and virtual-production services.

### Brand / Creative Support — supporting service
Includes visual/promotional assets and creative support. Avoid positioning DFB as a generic cheap logo shop; this capability is strongest when connected to media, web, software, or marketing systems.

### Transportation — active sellable service
DFB Transportation is a separate operational service line with pricing driven by vehicle, fuel, tolls, time, waiting, route complexity, liability, and logistics.

### Physical/property work — available capability, not core marketing focus
DFB has completed practical work such as decks/platforms and drywall repair. FREEOS should know this capability exists but not make it a primary front-facing category unless Drew intentionally expands it.

### Business consulting / problem solving — embedded capability
DFB often diagnoses workflow, technology, content, marketing, or operational problems. The strongest value usually comes when DFB implements the solution rather than only advising.

## Target customers

Primary target: small and growing businesses that are already operating but rely heavily on manual processes, texts/DMs, spreadsheets, paper, disconnected software, inconsistent marketing, or owner memory.

Strong profiles include:
- local service businesses,
- businesses drowning in repetitive administration,
- businesses with disconnected systems,
- companies without internal technical staff,
- growing businesses experiencing operational pain,
- businesses needing both technology and content,
- AI-curious owners who describe workflow pain rather than asking for "AI",
- businesses already paying labor to do repetitive work,
- event-related businesses,
- creators/artists with sufficient budget/strategic value,
- organizations needing custom internal software,
- businesses with outdated sites tied to real conversion/operational problems,
- existing DFB clients and referrals,
- repeat-business/retainer prospects,
- clients that can become measurable case studies.

Ideal AI/automation customer:
operating business + clear repeating manual process + real time/error cost + owner wants improvement + realistic DFB solution + value exceeds implementation cost.

Ideal overall customer: real problem, sufficient budget, clear decision-maker, respect for DFB expertise, and potential for strong margin, repeat revenue, referrals, strategic proof, or long-term relationship value.

Avoid or scrutinize: extreme price-only buyers, impossible expectations, vague scope, unpaid additions, exposure-as-payment, unclear ownership, and months of custom software without clear need/budget/adoption plan.

Technical services are not geographically limited in the same way as media/physical/transportation work. Local relationships can be a starting advantage while digital services expand farther.

## Pricing philosophy

Historical prices are evidence, not ceilings. As quality, capability, demand, equipment, and professionalism improve, pricing should evolve.

Pricing should account for direct costs, Drew's time, collaborator labor, equipment/software, travel, fulfillment, risk, overhead, and profit. Drew's time is not free.

Value can exceed labor time. Do not price a high-value automation purely because coding took a few hours.

Use the right pricing model: fixed project, package, hourly/day rate, retainer, recurring support, subscription/usage, licensing, setup + monthly, or milestone payments.

Prefer packaged pricing for predictable work and custom quotes for high variability.

Scope drives price: deliverables, duration, complexity, revisions, turnaround, travel, locations, users, integrations, support, and ownership/licensing.

Control scope creep. Rush work normally costs more. Deposits and milestone payments should reduce risk. Recurring support should have recurring pricing rather than indefinite free maintenance.

Friend/lookout pricing should be explicit:
standard commercial value -> owner-approved relationship discount -> final price. Discounted jobs must not silently redefine the normal rate.

Track quote, accepted price, direct cost, estimated/actual hours, revisions, travel, effective return, repeat/referral outcome, and profitability. Use real history to improve pricing.

DFB should charge enough that good work is worth doing well.

## Sales process

Pipeline:
NEW LEAD -> QUALIFIED -> DISCOVERY -> PROPOSAL SENT -> FOLLOW-UP -> WON/LOST -> DEPOSIT -> ACTIVE PROJECT -> DELIVERY -> FINAL PAYMENT -> TESTIMONIAL/REFERRAL -> REPEAT/RETAINER.

Lead sources may include referrals, existing clients, social media, website, direct outreach, networking, past relationships, local reputation, and partnerships.

Qualify before over-investing: need, fit, budget/timeline where appropriate, decision-maker, urgency, margin, repeat potential, strategic/portfolio value, and opportunity cost.

Discovery should diagnose the real problem rather than blindly selling the first requested deliverable. A request for a website may really be a lead-capture problem; a request for AI may really be repetitive admin; a video request may really need a repeatable content system.

Proposals should clearly show problem, solution, deliverables, timeline, price, deposit/payment structure, scope exclusions, ownership, revisions, and useful add-ons.

Track follow-up. Leads eventually become WON, LOST, PAUSED, NO RESPONSE, or NOT A FIT. Record meaningful loss reasons.

Before significant work, clarify agreement and collect the required deposit/first payment where appropriate.

Onboarding should gather required assets/access/requirements in one organized process rather than scattered requests.

FREEOS should progressively prepare discovery summaries, quotes, proposals, templates, onboarding, follow-ups, invoices, and project setup while preserving owner judgment for important decisions.

## Fulfillment standards

Delivery starts from the agreement. Translate client goal -> deliverables -> milestones -> tasks -> owner -> QC -> delivery.

Define acceptance criteria. Use checklists/templates for repeatable work while preserving necessary customization.

Verify prerequisites early: addresses, files, assets, equipment, credentials/access, requirements, deposits, approvals, dates.

One owner per deliverable. Automate repeatable administration but not judgment blindly.

Protect source material and keep backups of irreplaceable footage, code, source files, client data, and final deliverables. Track versions and canonical finals.

Centralize actionable client feedback. Distinguish defects, included subjective revisions, and out-of-scope change requests. Preserve revision history.

Quality-control the actual final artifact: test links, deployed sites/software, final video/audio, required branding/spelling/functionality.

Professional delivery should state what was delivered, where it is, how to access/use it, what remains, and what support is included.

Communicate delays early; record client-caused delays separately. Meet agreed quality without endless perfectionism.

Document and train for software/automation handoff when needed. Avoid single points of failure such as one undocumented login, local file, or owner memory.

Closeout should confirm deliverables/payment, organize/archive files, preserve authorized reusable assets, remove unnecessary temporary access, capture economics/lessons, and identify legitimate recurring opportunities.

Every completed project should make the next similar project easier, faster, more predictable, or more profitable.

Show the problem, show the solution, prove DFB can deliver, make the next step obvious.

## Internal operations

Every lead belongs in one CRM-like system with contact, source, problem, service relevance, estimated value, stage, last contact, next action, owner, notes, probability.

Each client should have one institutional home for identity, relationship, work history, quotes/invoices/payments, preferences, assets, systems built, opportunities, issues, and ownership.

Every won job becomes a formal project containing objective, scope, price, deposit/final payment, deadline, deliverables, milestones, client requirements, tasks, revisions, ownership, and status.

Build SOPs from proven workflows rather than writing giant manuals in advance. Priority SOPs: sales, media, websites, software, automation, transportation, finance, client management, assets.

Use predictable project file structures and track canonical/source/final versions and ownership.

Build pricing intelligence from actual jobs. Maintain operational finance visibility across collected, invoiced, contracted, recurring, pipeline, expenses, receivables, and project profitability.

## Team and delegation

## Initial 90-day business build direction

Days 1-30: organize services, website positioning, offer records, client history, CRM/pipeline, quote/proposal/invoice tracking, project templates, fulfillment economics, and existing-client recontact candidates.

Days 31-60: build revenue engine with strongest customer segments, automation/business-system audit offer, recurring content offer, managed support, case-study content, targeted outreach, referral network, systematic follow-up, and conversion measurement.
